## 0. Why Games Room exists (V0.105 amendment)

### The problem

Most adult socialising is a room full of people standing around with a drink
in hand, expected to strike up conversation from nothing. There is no shared
activity, no natural opener, no reason to be next to anyone in particular. The
whole social load sits on cold, unstructured talk, so the talk sinks to the
safest common denominator: small talk, work, weather, brain-dead chat about
whatever.

That format fails two kinds of people at once:

- **The person who is too in their own head.** When nothing occupies the hands
  or part of the mind, all attention turns inward onto "what do I say next".
  The same person is at ease across a table-tennis table, a poker hand or a
  blackjack shoe, because part of the mind is already busy and the game
  supplies the pauses, the openers and the shared reference point. (Side-by-side
  beats face-to-face; the drink in hand is itself a fidget-and-pause tool
  standing in for an activity.)
- **The person who wants a better conversation.** The conversations worth
  having, ideas, books, podcasts, arguments that swing from the serious to the
  completely unhinged and back, need familiar faces, repeated nights and a
  container that gives permission for both modes. A party gives neither.

Online substitutes (feeds, group chats, Discords) answer this by replacing the
room. Games Room answers it by giving the room a reason to exist.

### What Games Room is for

**Come for the games, stay for the people.** The games are the excuse and the
scaffold; the conversation and the community are the point.

Goals, in order:

1. **The game is social scaffolding.** Hands busy, mind free. Turns create
   natural pauses, the table creates side-by-side seating, the stakes create
   instant shared stories. Nobody has to perform an opener.
2. **Raise the ceiling of the conversation.** The target register is a
   hangout podcast at a card table (Modern Wisdom's hangout episodes are the
   reference): able to oscillate freely between real intellectual substance
   and unhinged goofiness, and neither mode is out of place. The competitive
   game supplies the goofy energy; the lulls between hands supply room for the
   deep stuff.
3. **A refined club, not a party.** Membership, ritual, a recurring seat, a
   house character, a season arc. The feel of a members' games room, not a
   kitchen party or a bar.
4. **A community of people you want to think with.** Over a season, a room
   becomes a standing group of friends-of-friends you can discuss ideas with,
   the people you would want on the other mic.
5. **Technology serves the room, never replaces it.** Everything in §1–§5 holds.
   Any conversation feature lives before the night (briefing), between games
   (host surface, intermissions) or after (recap). Never on members' phones
   during play.

### North star

> **Come for the games. Stay for the conversation.**
> Games Room plans the night and sets the table for good conversation, then
> gets out of the way so the room can do the rest.

The V0.53 line below remains the operating rule for *how*: the app plans the
night so you can live it.

### Product implications (candidates, not commitments)

Ranked. Each must pass the §4 holding test before it is specced.

1. **Question box at RSVP.** Each member can drop one question with their RSVP
   (serious or ridiculous). The host gets the pool on the iPad and pulls one
   between games. Members bring the conversation; the app just carries it.
2. **Conversation access points in the briefing.** One optional "currently
   into" line per member (a book, a podcast, an obsession), shown in the T-48h
   briefing next to who is coming. Replaces "so what do you do?" with something
   to actually talk about.
3. **Intermissions with a dial.** The host surface marks breaks between games
   and offers one prompt from a deck tuned on two axes, *deep* and *unhinged*.
   The room oscillates; the host sets the rhythm. One prompt, optional, never
   pushed to phones.
4. **Line of the night.** The recap gains one host-captured line (best idea,
   best take, best joke) alongside the scores, so the ledger remembers what was
   said, not just who won.
5. **Room charter.** At room-create the host writes the house rules in a few
   lines (e.g. "phones face down, no work talk before the second game, any topic
   is fair game"). The club's character, stated once, shown on the chair card.

What not to build: icebreaker games on members' phones, a discussion feed,
topic threads, comments on recaps. Each turns the conversation into an app
surface, which is the failure §4 names.

## 1. The anti-thesis

**Games Room is the counter-trend to social media.**

The social-media bet of the last fifteen years is that technology can
_replace_ the social interaction: feeds stand in for conversation, likes
stand in for warmth, group chats stand in for a group in a room. Every
success metric in that world is measured in time in the feed. The result
is a loneliness epidemic, a friendship recession, and a generation that
has lost the muscle for in-person coordination.

Games Room makes the opposite bet: **technology should _facilitate_ the
social interaction, not replace it.** The app gets people to a real
table. Then it gets out of the way.

The job splits cleanly in two:

- **The app plans the night.** It kills the friction of organising one —
  the date, the venue, the RSVPs, the scores, the arc. This is the part
  that dies in group chats today.
- **The app does not run the night.** Players are physically present at
  the table. The social lives in the room, not on the screen. During
  play the app is quiet, or absent.

The North Star that follows from this:

> **Games Room plans the night so you can live it. The app brings the
> people to the table, then gets out of the way.**

## 2. Why now

- The third place died in 2019 and hasn't come back. Pubs are
  restaurants, cafés are co-working, and the free low-stakes
  "just hanging out" venue is priced out almost everywhere.
- Group chats replaced the group. The friction of physical coordination
  — date, venue, who's in, what we're playing, what do I bring — is now
  the entire reason most nights die in the iMessage thread.
- Digital fatigue has created a premium on physical presence. People are
  tired of being online but have lost the muscle for in-person
  coordination. A tool that makes the physical thing *easy* is worth more
  now than it was in 2018, when the friction was tolerable.
- The loneliness epidemic is real, and male friendship specifically is in
  collapse. Flake is the disease. Games Room's deposit-forfeit mechanism
  is structurally anti-flake: it makes showing up the default.

Games Room doesn't invent a new social need. It answers the one the
market broke: **people want to be in a room together, and the machinery
to get them there has been taken over by feeds that keep them apart.**

## 3. The design principles

These are the shape of the product. They survive scope changes.

1. **Tech facilitates, doesn't replace.** The app plans the night; the
   room runs it. Any feature that simulates the interaction is suspect.
2. **Runs off-stage.** The phone is invisible during play. The mascot is
   silent while the game is live; **mascot absence is the feature.**
3. **Kills friction, not the social.** The app does the bookkeeping, the
   invitations, the RSVPs, the scores. The room does the talking.
4. **The ledger is the durable social.** Most games nights have no
   memory. Competitive friendship needs an arc, not isolated evenings.
   The ledger is what members brag about outside the app, without the
   app on-screen.
5. **Quiet by default.** The app is most useful on game night and quiet
   the rest of the time. Default opens: tonight's room, my hand, this
   week's standings. Nothing else.
6. **The host is the customer.** The host does the highest-cost work —
   generating the social surface in real time. The dashboard's job is to
   do that *for* the host (a narrator voice), not to pile on bookkeeping.
7. **The members are at the table, not in the app.** The product's job
   during play is mostly to not get in the way. Its job before and after
   is to make the social possible.
8. **Trusted by design.** Invite-only. No marketplace, no public feed, no
   DMs. Privacy is the product, not a setting.
9. **Craft over numbers.** Small, correct, durable. Slow compounding over
   growth-hacks. The polish is the work.
10. **The Jiro principle.** The engineering can be complex; the customer
    experience is simple. Every layer of internal complexity buys a layer
    of surface calm.

## 4. What we are NOT building

These are hard lines. If a feature crosses one, it is not Games Room.

- **No new feed or notification stream.** No infinite scroll, no
  "what's happening" timeline, no engagement graph. The only pushes are
  the three that make the night happen (on-create, T-48h, morning-of),
  and they are logistics, not bait.
- **No public marketplace, no public DMs.** Rooms are invite-only.
  Members connect through the room, never through a global directory.
- **Nothing that rewards more time in the app.** No streaks, no
  points-for-the-app, no "daily check-in." The metric that matters is
  nights attended, not minutes in the feed.
- **Nothing that turns the room into a livestream of itself.** No live
  spectator mode, no "watch the night unfold" surface. If you're not at
  the table, you get the recap, not the stream.

**The one line to hold:** *if a feature keeps a user looking at the
screen instead of looking at the people across the table, it fails the
product.*

---

## 5. The long arc

Games Room compounds. Each night deepens the season arc; each season
deepens the friendship. The relationships are the product, and the
product is a recurring weekly habit that ends with people *more*
connected, not more online. That is the counter-trend: an app whose
success is measured by how little it is needed while the people it
brought together are together.

## Slogan candidates (anchored to the counter-trend)

Ranked by fit to the framing above and the established landing voice
("You bring your people. Games Room plans the night, runs the scores,
and stays quiet during play.").

1. **"Plans the night. Doesn't run it."** — the purest statement of the
   anti-thesis. Short, active, says exactly what the app does and what
   it refuses to do.
2. **"Get the people to the table. Then get out of the way."** — the
   two-job split verbatim. Warm, human, slightly longer.
3. **"Less phone. More table."** — the counter-trend in four words.
   Punchy, memorable, and it names the enemy without naming a company.
4. **"Your people. Your table. Your arc."** — the durable-social frame.
   Ties to "Your games night, counted." Strong for the ledger/season
   story.
5. **"We do the bookkeeping. You do the night."** — the friction-killer
   frame. Plain, honest, no hype. Fits the app-store description voice.
